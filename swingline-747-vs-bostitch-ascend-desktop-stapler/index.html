Head-to-head desktop stapler comparison

Swingline 747 vs Bostitch Ascend

Swingline’s 747 Classic S7074769 is a 20-sheet, full-strip metal desktop stapler with a die-cast base, tacking and pinning functions, and a stated limited lifetime warranty. Bostitch’s Ascend B210-BLK staples 2 to 20 sheets and adds a soft grip, built-in staple remover, StapleStore compartment, reload window, non-skid base, and 180-degree tacking. The useful choice is about durable classic hardware versus convenience at the desk.

The Swingline 747 Classic is the better all-around choice for a desk that needs a familiar, full-strip stapler without adding extra mechanisms. For the steel-gray S7074769 model, Swingline specifies a 20-sheet capacity, durable metal construction with a die-cast base, full-strip size, tacking ability, pinning ability, and a positive-locking latch. It also lists a limited lifetime warranty. Those are direct ownership details for a buyer who wants a traditional desktop tool with clearly described functions.

The Bostitch Ascend B210-BLK has a real convenience advantage. Bostitch lists a 2-to-20-sheet capacity and a 210-staple magazine, then adds an integrated StapleStore compartment with 420 included staples, a built-in remover, a visual reload alert window, a non-skid base, and an ergonomic soft grip and base. It opens 180 degrees for tacking or craft use. Choose it when the desk routine benefits more from those integrated helpers than from the Swingline’s metal, full-strip classic format.

Because both exact models publish a 20-sheet maximum, capacity does not decide this one. The deciding trade-off is construction and classic office functions against convenience features that reduce small interruptions. The Swingline is the more durable-looking, simpler default on the published specifications. The Ascend is the more purposeful alternative for someone who routinely loses a remover, wants spare staples close by, or prefers a softer hand contact.

How we reasoned

The criteria behind the pick.

This comparison starts with the one numerical point that should not be exaggerated: both exact products publish a 20-sheet maximum. Swingline identifies the S7074769 as a 20-sheet 747 Classic. Bostitch lists the B210-BLK capacity as 2 to 20 sheets. That means the choice is not a simple capacity contest. It is a decision about how the stapler stores, reloads, feels, and handles the small tasks around a stack of paper.

We gave the 747 the overall recommendation because Swingline provides a clear classic-hardware case: metal construction, a die-cast base, full-strip format, tacking, pinning, a positive-locking latch, and limited lifetime warranty wording. For many buyers, that is the right kind of clarity. There is less to interpret and fewer integrated parts to account for. A full-strip metal desktop stapler also fits the familiar rhythm of a permanent office or home desk.

The Ascend earns its alternative recommendation because Bostitch documents features that change the immediate routine. Its StaplesStore compartment keeps extra staples close, and its built-in remover avoids a separate tool. The visual reload alert window and non-skid base are small but practical details. The soft grip and 180-degree opening make its case stronger for mixed desk-and-project use. These are features, not proof that it staples more cleanly or lasts longer in every environment.

We did not infer jam rates, hand-force reduction, staple quality, longevity beyond stated materials and warranty, or a particular result with cardstock, recycled paper, or bulletin boards. The product pages establish functions and capacities, not universal real-world guarantees. Choose by the published details that match your own desk, paper volume, storage habits, and preference for metal hardware or integrated helpers.

There is also a useful ownership question that does not fit into a sheet-capacity number: where do the accessories live? A full strip of staples and a separate remover are ordinary desk supplies, but they can be inconvenient in a shallow drawer, a rolling classroom cart, or a shared work surface. Bostitch deliberately combines those pieces in the Ascend. The 747 makes the opposite kind of case: conventional metal construction, a familiar loading format, and stated pinning as well as tacking. Neither approach is inherently superior; it simply changes what needs to be kept nearby.

Which desktop stapler is the better pick for most people?+

The Swingline 747 is the better pick for most people choosing between these two desktop staplers. Swingline specifies a metal construction with a die-cast base, a full-strip format, tacking and pinning ability, and a limited lifetime warranty. Choose the Bostitch Ascend when its integrated StapleStore compartment, built-in remover, soft grip, reload alert window, and 20-sheet stated capacity better match a compact, convenience-first desk routine.

Do the Swingline 747 and Bostitch Ascend have the same sheet capacity?+

For the exact models compared here, both manufacturers publish a 20-sheet capacity. Swingline identifies the S7074769 as a 20-sheet 747 Classic, while Bostitch lists the B210-BLK capacity as 2 to 20 sheets. Sheet count is a manufacturer specification; paper stock, staple selection, and document condition can affect everyday results.

Which stapler is better for keeping spare staples at the desk?+

Bostitch gives the Ascend B210-BLK a direct convenience advantage: its official page describes an integrated StapleStore compartment and says it includes 420 staples there. It also lists a 210-staple magazine capacity. The Swingline 747 is a full-strip desktop stapler, but its official product page does not claim the Bostitch-style built-in staple-storage compartment.

Can both staplers tack papers to a board?+

Yes. Swingline lists tacking ability for the 747 Classic. Bostitch says the Ascend opens 180 degrees for tacking or craft use. Those published features describe the open-stapler function; they do not guarantee a result on a particular wall, board, material, or project.

What is the practical difference between the two models?+

The Swingline 747 is the metal, full-strip classic with a die-cast base, tacking and pinning functions, positive-locking latch, and limited lifetime warranty. The Bostitch Ascend is the lightweight convenience option with a soft grip and base, built-in remover, staple-storage compartment, reload window, non-skid base, and a stated 20-sheet capacity. Pick by desk workflow rather than assuming either design is universally better.
